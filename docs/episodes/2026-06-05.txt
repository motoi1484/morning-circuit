Let's shift gears to security and policy, where there was a significant legal decision. The Supreme Court ruled against AT&T and Verizon — two of the biggest names in American telecom — in a case over fines for selling customers' location data. This goes back to the practice of carriers selling access to data about where their customers physically were. The carriers had argued, in part, that the way the Federal Communications Commission fined them violated their right to a jury trial. The court disagreed, in an eight-to-one ruling, finding the FCC did not violate that right. So the fines stand. This matters because your location data is some of the most sensitive information about you — it can reveal where you live, where you work, where you worship, who you visit. The idea that carriers were selling access to that, and are now being held to account, is a meaningful win for privacy. Eight-to-one is also a pretty decisive margin, which makes a strong statement about where the law lands on this.

Staying with your personal security — a cautionary tale from the password manager Dashlane. The company has explained how attackers managed to download encrypted password vaults from some of its users. Now, the important nuance: the vaults are encrypted, so the attackers grabbing them isn't the same as them instantly reading your passwords. But here's the clever, nasty part of the attack — by targeting a very large number of users at once, the attackers improved their overall odds of success. Even if only a small fraction of those vaults could eventually be cracked, at a big enough scale, a small fraction is still a lot of people. The lesson for all of us is the same boring advice that happens to be true: use a strong, unique master password that isn't reused anywhere else, and turn on multi-factor authentication wherever you can. Encryption is your friend, but it works a lot better when your master password isn't your dog's name and a one.
