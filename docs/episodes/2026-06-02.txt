Now what's actually happening here is a deep shift in how cars get made, even if you never see it. Designing a vehicle involves an enormous amount of simulation — things like computational fluid dynamics, which is how you model airflow over a car's body, and finite element analysis, which is how you model how parts hold up under stress and strain. Traditionally these simulations are brutally slow; you set up a scenario, you let powerful computers grind on it for hours, and then you look at the results. Now GM is layering AI and machine learning on top of all that, along with what are called digital twins — full virtual replicas of vehicles and components that engineers can test against in software. The payoff is speed. When a simulation that used to eat fifteen hours now takes one minute, you don't just save time — you change what's possible. You can test dozens of design ideas in the time it used to take to test one. You can iterate, experiment, fail fast, and improve in ways that just weren't feasible before. The cars of the next few years are increasingly being designed, tested, and refined in virtual space long before any metal gets bent. It's a quiet revolution, but it's a real one.
