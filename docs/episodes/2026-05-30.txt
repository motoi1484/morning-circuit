TechCrunch also ran a conversation featuring Aaron Levie — he's the founder of Box — and he used a phrase that stuck with me: "AI psychosis." His point was this. The people deciding that AI can replace your job are very often the people who are least likely to actually understand what your job involves day to day. The executive looking at a spreadsheet sees a function. They don't see the judgment, the context, the thousand small decisions that the person in that role makes. And so they conclude, a little too eagerly, that an AI agent can simply slot in.

ClickUp, the productivity software company, recently cut twenty-two percent of its workforce — more than one in five people — and pointed to AI agents as part of the rationale. So whatever you think about the technology, the human cost of the AI narrative is landing in real time, in real paychecks. And the worry Levie is putting his finger on is that some of these cuts are being made on a fantasy version of what the software can actually do — and that companies that gut their teams now may find out the hard way that the agent couldn't quite carry the load.

Here's where it gets genuinely interesting, though, because the people building these tools are not necessarily the ones promising they'll replace you. Cognition is the company behind Devin — which was, depending on who you ask, the first and arguably the most successful AI coding agent. The kind of tool that, on paper, sounds like it exists to put programmers out of work. That's a remarkable thing to hear from the person selling the agent. His framing is that the tool is there to handle the grind, the repetitive scaffolding, so that the humans can focus on the harder, more creative parts of building software. So you've got a real gap opening up between the sales pitch as understood in the boardroom — "this replaces headcount" — and the pitch as the builders actually intend it — "this makes your good people faster."

And there's a twist to that story that should make every engineer sit up. There's research out, also covered by TechCrunch, suggesting that coders are increasingly refusing to work without AI assistance. They've gotten so used to having an AI copilot generate code for them that working without it feels like writing with one hand tied behind their back. Sounds great for productivity, right? Here's the catch the researchers are raising. AI is helping coders produce code faster — but faster is not the same as better. The concern is that all this machine-generated code may be lower quality, harder to maintain, riddled with subtle issues that nobody fully understands because nobody fully wrote it. And if an entire generation of developers loses the muscle memory of doing it themselves — of reasoning through a problem from scratch — then when something breaks in a way the AI can't fix, who's left who actually knows how the engine works? That's a long-term bet on dependency, and it could absolutely come back to bite the very people relying on it. So you've got speed today, potentially a skills cliff tomorrow. Worth keeping an eye on.

And speaking of training the machines, here's a story that sits right at the border between AI and robotics, and it's a perfect transition. It's clever, and it's also a little unsettling when you sit with it. All right, let's shift gears into the world of security and the bigger institutional stories, because there were some heavy hitters yesterday.

First, a real dust-up in the security community. Microsoft is under fire — and taking some serious criticism — for reportedly threatening an independent security researcher with a criminal investigation. Now, this touches a very old and very raw nerve in the IT world. The whole ecosystem of software security depends on independent researchers — people who poke at systems, find vulnerabilities, and responsibly report them so they can be fixed before the bad guys find them. That's the deal. It's how the internet stays standing. So when one of the largest software companies on earth responds to a researcher not with a thank-you and a bug bounty but with the threat of legal jeopardy, it sends a chilling message to everyone in that field: keep your head down, don't look too closely, because if you do, you might get a lawyer's letter instead of a handshake. The piece frames this as reopening a long-running debate over who is actually responsible for securing software — the vendor who shipped it, or the outsider who found the hole. And reasonable people can argue the details of this specific case. But the broader principle matters enormously. If you scare off the good-faith researchers, you don't make the software safer. You just make sure the only people looking for the flaws are the ones who'd never tell you.

On the brighter side of security, there was a genuine win. Authorities dismantled a botnet made up of more than seventeen million devices. Seventeen million. To put that in perspective — that's a vast, sprawling network of compromised gadgets, routers, smart-home devices, all quietly conscripted into criminal service without their owners ever knowing. This particular botnet was reportedly tied to a Russia-based residential proxy network. And here's why that's significant: a residential proxy network lets criminals route their traffic through ordinary home internet connections — yours, potentially — so that their activity looks like it's coming from a normal house in a normal neighborhood, rather than from some shady server farm. That makes fraud, credential stuffing, and all manner of bad behavior much harder to detect and block. Taking down a network of this scale is a serious blow, and a reminder of an uncomfortable truth: the cheap smart device you bought and forgot about might be doing someone else's dirty work right now. Update your firmware, change those default passwords. It matters more than it feels like it does.

Okay, let's come up for air and talk about the platforms we actually use every day, because there's a fascinating strategic story here.

TechCrunch has a piece on TikTok's road to becoming a so-called super app. Now, "super app" is a concept that's been huge in Asia for years — think of an app like WeChat, where you don't just message friends, you also pay your bills, hail a cab, order food, book a doctor, and shop, all without ever leaving. One app to rule them all. And TikTok, it appears, wants to be exactly that for the rest of the world. It started as a place for short videos, but you can see the pieces being assembled — shopping through TikTok Shop, payments, an ever-expanding ecosystem of things to do inside the app. The strategy makes total sense from their side: the longer they keep you inside their walls, the more of your digital life they own, and the more money they make. From our side, as users, it's a double-edged sword. Convenience is real — there's something genuinely nice about not bouncing between fifteen apps. But there's also a concentration of power and data in a single company that should give us pause, especially one that's already been the subject of so much geopolitical wrangling. The everything-app dream is seductive. It's also a lot of eggs in one basket.
