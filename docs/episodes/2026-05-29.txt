But before we get too swept up in the hype, let me bring you back to earth with a genuinely sobering piece of research. A study found that large language models — the AI systems behind all these products — will believe false statements even after being explicitly warned that those statements are false. The researchers ran fine-tuning tests and found what they described as a bias toward confidently representing claims as true. Let that sink in. You can tell the model, in plain language, "this thing I'm about to tell you is false," and the model can still internalize it and then confidently repeat it as fact. This is the heart of why I keep urging caution when people treat these tools as oracles. They are extraordinarily capable at producing fluent, confident-sounding text. They are not, in any reliable sense, evaluating whether what they're saying is true. Confidence and accuracy are two completely different things, and these systems have an abundance of the first and an unreliable supply of the second. Keep that in mind every time one of them gives you an answer that sounds authoritative.

Let's pivot now to security and crime, because there were a few stories yesterday that deserve a heads-up — and the first one is genuinely actionable, so if you use Signal, please listen up.

There's a new wave of widespread phishing attacks targeting Signal users. Signal, for those who don't know, is the encrypted messaging app favored by people who care a lot about privacy. The attackers are trying to trick users into giving up their secret recovery key. That recovery key is the thing that can be used to access your online backups — which contain your past messages. So the whole value of Signal, the privacy of your conversation history, hinges on keeping that key secret. The takeaway is simple but important: your recovery key is not something you ever share, type into a website, or hand to anyone, no matter how official the request looks. Legitimate services do not ask you to enter your recovery key into a random link. If you get a message or a prompt urging you to do that, treat it as an attack. Slow down, don't click, and verify through official channels. Phishing works because it creates urgency and catches you off guard during your busy day — which, ironically, is exactly when many of you are listening to this. So consider this your reminder to stay skeptical.

The second security story is one of those tales that's almost too on-the-nose for our era. The FBI says a Google engineer used internal company search data to win one point two million dollars on Polymarket. Polymarket, if you're not familiar, is a prediction market — a platform where people bet real money on the outcomes of future events. According to the FBI, this engineer had access to internal Google search data that essentially told him the outcomes of certain bets in advance, and he used that knowledge to place wager after wager, profiting one point two million dollars. It's insider trading for the prediction-market age. Instead of trading on secret knowledge of a company's earnings, the allegation is that he traded on secret knowledge of what the world was searching for — and therefore what was about to happen or be revealed. It's a fascinating illustration of how much predictive power is locked inside a company like Google, and how that power can be abused. And it's a reminder that the people with access to the most sensitive data are always going to face the temptation to monetize it. The case is a warning shot, both for prediction markets and for the companies sitting on mountains of behavioral data.

And the third security story is my favorite, because it's a little act of rebellion. A developer, apparently fed up with so-called "vibe coders," snuck a data-nuking prompt injection into their own code. Let me unpack that. "Vibe coding" is the somewhat derisive term for people who lean heavily on AI coding assistants to generate software, often without fully understanding what the code does. This developer maintains a tool called jqwik, and they quietly added a hidden instruction — a prompt injection — designed to be read by AI coding agents. The instruction told those AI agents to delete the application's output. So if you were using an AI agent to work with this code without reading it carefully, the agent could be tricked into wiping your results. Now, I want to be clear — that's an aggressive and arguably reckless thing to do, because it can harm innocent users. But it's also a pointed statement about a growing tension in software development. As more people lean on AI to write code they don't understand, the people who actually maintain the underlying tools are getting frustrated. This is a flare being sent up over a real conflict in the developer community, about craft, responsibility, and what happens when you trust a machine to do work you can't check. Don't try this at home — but do pay attention to what it's telling us.
